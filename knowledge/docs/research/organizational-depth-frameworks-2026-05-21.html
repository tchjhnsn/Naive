Prior-art research — Organizational depth & recursion frameworks

Industry-awareness protocol run on the question of whether an existing framework already exists for measuring nested organizational depth and recursion, where each nested level may have its own pillar decomposition.

Research Drafted v1 2026-05-21

The short answer: Yes, prior art exists — but no single framework fully fits. Five candidates investigated. Recommendation: borrow primitives from VSM (recursion) and TOGAF (ontology), borrow vocabulary from holding-company taxonomy, and build the methodology-specific layer (three pillars, four rings, four threads) ourselves.

2. Prior-art surface — five frameworks

1. Viable System Model (VSM) — Stafford Beer, 1972+

What it does: A cybernetic framework describing any viable system as five interacting subsystems: S1 (operations), S2 (coordination), S3 (control + 3* audit), S4 (intelligence), S5 (policy/identity). The defining property: VSM is explicitly recursive — viable systems contain viable systems, modeled with the same cybernetic description at every level.

Audience: Cybernetics-informed organizational theorists, systems thinkers, complex-organization consultants, increasingly enterprise AI architects.

Recent assessment: Experiencing a renaissance in the AI era — multiple Medium articles in 2025-2026 apply VSM to "autonomous AI organizations."

Fit for our need: Closest cousin to what we want. The recursion principle is a perfect match.

2. TOGAF + ArchiMate — The Open Group, ongoing

What it does: TOGAF is an enterprise architecture framework with an Architecture Development Method (ADM) that is explicitly iterative and recursive. ArchiMate is the modeling language with a formal ontology for organizational architectures.

Audience: Enterprise IT architects, large-organization transformation consultants, GRC teams.

Recent assessment (2025): Industry research is increasingly using ontology-driven enterprise architecture aligned with TOGAF/ArchiMate.

Fit for our need: Closest cousin for the ontology approach. Too heavyweight to adopt wholesale, but the ontology-driven philosophy is exactly what we need.

3. Holding company hierarchy taxonomy — long-established

What it does: Provides legal and financial vocabulary for nested corporate structures: parent company → subsidiary → intermediate company → operating company → division → business unit.

Audience: Corporate lawyers, CFOs, M&A teams, governance.

Fit for our need: Useful for frontmatter vocabulary. Add fields like parent-entity-legal-type and subsidiary-status directly.

4. Divisional (M-form) structure — Alfred Chandler, 1962+

What it does: Describes the multi-business corporate structure where each division has its own P&L, GM, cross-functional team, and clear scope.

Audience: Management consultants, corporate strategists, multi-business orgs.

Fit for our need: Useful for distinguishing scopes. Helps tell apart "division of existing entity" vs "separate subsidiary entity."

5. Organizational depth — management literature

What it does: Measures hierarchical layers between CEO and front-line employees. Used in flat-vs-deep org design.

Fit for our need: Borrowed term we should disambiguate from. Industry's "organizational depth" is about management layers, not entity nesting. Worth flagging.

3. Build-vs-adopt analysis

Should we adopt one of these wholesale? No. None is a complete fit.

  • VSM has the right recursion principle but the wrong decomposition (S1–S5 ≠ product/brand/business)
  • TOGAF/ArchiMate has the right ontology approach but is too heavyweight and IT-focused
  • Holding company taxonomy is good vocabulary but isn't a methodology
  • M-form is descriptive, not prescriptive
  • Organizational depth measures a different thing entirely

Should we build entirely from scratch? Also no. We'd be reinventing primitives that already exist.

Recommended hybrid

  1. Borrow from VSM — the principle that recursive entities contain recursive entities, modeled with identical structure at every level.
  2. Borrow from TOGAF/ArchiMate — the ontology-driven modeling philosophy. Make our schema explicit, formal, queryable.
  3. Borrow from holding-company taxonomy — legal/financial nesting vocabulary for frontmatter. Add parent-entity-legal-type and subsidiary-status fields.
  4. Build the methodology-specific layer — three-pillar decomposition, four-ring × four-thread weave, substrate-invariance practice. None of the borrowed frameworks has these.
  5. Disambiguate "depth" — call our concept entity recursion depth to avoid collision.

4. Implication for a consulting practice

The schema-modeling exercises (Apple, Berkshire, Disney, Toyota, Alphabet) become VSM-aware and TOGAF-informed without being beholden to either. We test our schema against the same complexity those frameworks handle, but we keep our methodology's three-pillar / four-ring / four-thread structure intact.

This makes the "borrow + build" recommendation defensible publicly: we are not reinventing organizational modeling, we are applying well-established primitives (VSM recursion, TOGAF ontology, holding-company vocabulary) to a specific methodology (three pillars, four rings, four threads, substrate-invariance) that has no prior-art equivalent.
